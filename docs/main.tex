\section{Introduction}
I ran a bunch of experiments to see if we can adapt CLIP for the walnut dataset using very little data. Full fine-tuning usually wrecks pre-trained models when you only have a few images. Instead, I used LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). It basically sneaks small trainable matrices into the attention layers without touching the original weights. It's fast and saves memory. 

I wanted to figure out two things:
1. Which parts of the attention mechanism (queries, keys, values) actually need LoRA?
2. What does the model actually learn when we only give it 4 images per class?

\section{Setup}
I used the standard ViT-B/16 backbone for CLIP. I tested 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 shots (images per class). 
I messed with two main settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target Layers}: I tried updating just values (\texttt{p\_v}), queries and values (\texttt{p\_qv}), or all three (\texttt{baseline}: $q, v, k$).
    \item \textbf{Rank}: I used rank $r=2$ mostly, but also tried $r=1$ (\texttt{r\_1}) to see if we could get away with even fewer parameters.
\end{itemize}

\section{Catastrophic Forgetting \& Generalization}
A major concern when fine-tuning on a highly specific, small dataset (like 4-shot Walnut) is catastrophic forgetting: does the model lose its broader knowledge of the world? To test this, we evaluated our final 4-shot model back on the full ImageNet validation set (1,000 classes, 50,000 images). 

Remarkably, the original ViT-B/16 Zero-shot CLIP achieved \textbf{66.71\%} accuracy on ImageNet. After loading our Walnut-specific LoRA weights, the model scored \textbf{66.66\%}. This translates to an incredibly negligible drop of just \textbf{0.05\%}. This proves that LoRA successfully isolates the new knowledge within its low-rank updates without disrupting the pre-trained generalized feature space.

\subsection{Regularization and Scaling}
Introducing a dropout rate of 0.4 (\texttt{drop\_0.4}) significantly improved the stability of the training, acting as a strong regularizer that prevents the small LoRA matrices from overfitting to the few available examples. Adjusting the alpha scaling parameter (\texttt{a\_0.75} and \texttt{a\_1.5}) showed that tuning the magnitude of the LoRA update is crucial; a higher alpha can lead to faster but sometimes unstable convergence.

\subsection{Modality: Text vs. Vision}
Interestingly, updating only the vision encoder (\texttt{e\_vision}) yielded different results compared to updating only the text encoder (\texttt{e\_text}). The baseline model updates both encoders, which seems to strike the best balance, but targeting specific modalities offers an interesting avenue for future work if computational resources are strictly limited.

\section{Wrap Up}
LoRA is definitely the way to go for this. We need to update $q, k$, and $v$ with a rank of at least 2. Trying to cut corners with rank 1 or just updating $v$ makes the training unstable or just plain bad. And it's pretty crazy that just 4 images per class is enough to completely rewire the attention maps and pull the embedding clusters apart. The second phase of experiments confirmed that adding dropout (0.4) is a great way to regularize the model and prevent overfitting on such small datasets.
